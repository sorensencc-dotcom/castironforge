## US2383225A — Aircraft Manufacture
**Filed:** Oct 24, 1942 | **Granted:** Aug 21, 1945
**Co-inventor:** Roscoe M. Smith (Ann Arbor, MI)
**Class:** B64F5/00 — Aircraft manufacturing

### The Explicit Automotive → Aircraft Transfer Statement

The first paragraph of the description contains Sorensen's own words connecting the automotive assembly line to Willow Run — documentary-grade primary source:

> "This invention relates to the art of aircraft construction and, more particularly, to a method and an apparatus for the **continuous assembly of aircraft**. While the **line assembly method has been used with singular success in the automotive industry** and in many other manufacturing operations, it is only recently that attempts have been made to adopt it for use in aircraft. This has been particularly difficult because of the complexity of the assembly procedure and the size of the objects to be assembled. Therefore, when attempts were made to adapt prior conventional methods of line assembly to aircraft construction, it was found that they were far from satisfactory. **The only solution appears to be to adopt a new method** differing in detail and content from those previously used and directed particularly to the problems encountered in aircraft assembly."

This is Sorensen, in a legal document, stating:
1. He invented a new method of continuous aircraft assembly
2. It builds directly on the automotive assembly line he pioneered
3. Prior attempts to adapt automotive methods to aircraft failed
4. His solution is architecturally different — not a copy, an invention

### The Method: Multi-Dimensional Assembly

The innovation is explicitly contrasted with conventional linear assembly:

> "In distinction to the conventional assembly practice, which is basically linear in that it entails the addition of minor component after component to a foundation structure as it progresses, the **present method is multidimensional** in that it unites a number of linear operations, resulting in major subassemblies which are thereafter mated into major assemblies and which are in turn concentrated from these prior lines for **independent rather than cumulative final operations**."

The structure:
- Four parallel major assembly lines
- Wing center sections move on wing section conveyors (hung from wingtips)
- Fuselage sections (bomb bay, nose, aft) arrive from separate subassembly lines
- Major subassemblies converge at stations A, B, C, D
- Transfer to two final assembly conveyors
- The B-24 progresses through tail empennage, propellers, outer wing sections, wing tips

### B-24 Identification — Scale and Structural Method

> "applied to the assembly of planes having wing spreads of **upwards of 100 feet**"

The B-24 Liberator has a 110-foot wingspan. No other aircraft in production at Willow Run matches this description.

The key structural claim: supporting the aircraft **solely by its wingtips** during fuselage assembly:

> "solely supporting said wing unit by the tips of said wings, leaving the remaining portions of the unit unsupported, and while said wing unit is so supported, assembling said fuselage sections with the central portion of said wing unit"

Sorensen's own note on why this matters:
> "It has the further advantage that any stresses encountered in the assembly are of the same nature as will be impressed upon the plane in flight. **No other suspension method achieves this.**"

By hanging the B-24 from its wingtips during assembly, the wing flexes under the load of the fuselage exactly as it will flex in flight — ensuring every riveted joint is stressed in the correct direction from the start. This is structural engineering insight applied to production, not just assembly logistics.

### Signed
> "CHARLES E. SORENSEN. ROSCOE M. SMITH."

Both inventors signed the claims. Roscoe M. Smith: Ann Arbor, Mich. address — local to Willow Run.

### Impact
- Filed Oct 24, 1942 — 5 weeks after the first B-24 rolled out of Willow Run
- 27 subsequent patent citations (highest across all Sorensen patents)
- Cited by Boeing's Determinant Wing Assembly (WO1997034734A1, 1997) — 55 years later
- Directly enabled scaling from ~1/week to 1/day to 1/hour production rate

## US2378043A — Method of Airframe Assembly
**Filed:** Oct 24, 1942 | **Granted:** Jun 12, 1945
**Co-inventor:** Roscoe M. Smith (Ann Arbor, MI)
**Class:** B64F5/10 — Manufacturing or assembling aircraft, e.g. jigs therefor

### What It Covers

Filed the same day as US2383225A. Where US2383225A covers the **factory-level assembly system** (four parallel lines, conveyor layout, station sequence), US2378043A covers the **component-level assembly method** for the wing section itself — the jigs, fixtures, and sequence for building a 40-foot B-24 wing section.

Key quote from description:
> "to devise an apparatus by the use of which **overall limits measured in thousandths of an inch may be maintained in structures upwardly of 40 feet in length**"

Maintaining thousandths-of-an-inch precision across 40-foot structures — this was the fundamental manufacturing challenge at Willow Run. Aircraft parts must be interchangeable; if wing sections from different shifts or different fixture sets don't match, the plane can't be assembled. This patent solves that interchangeability problem at scale.

### The Assembly Sequence (B-24 wing section)

Step A: Preform skin segments, form to required curvature
Step B: Rivet segments together with splice plates → complete skin
Step C: Rivet skin to longitudinal stringers → skin panel
Step D: Build wing skeleton — spars + bulkheads — in separate fixture
Step E: Lower skin panel onto skeleton, bolt stringers to bulkheads

Key insight: riveting (permanent, irreversible) is done in stages when access is unobstructed. Final attachment (skin to skeleton) uses bolts — allowing final alignment before committing. This sequences operations to preserve access and prevent trapped-work situations at scale.

### Boeing Citation — 1997

US2378043A was cited in:
- **Boeing WO1997034734A1** — "Determinant Wing Assembly" (1997) — the engineering process that defines modern commercial aircraft assembly
- Boeing WO2004020286A1 — "Single Piece Flow Based Wing Assembly System" (2002)
- Boeing US5033178A — "Assembly Jig and Method for Making Wing Panels" (1991)

Sorensen's 1942 Willow Run wing assembly method was foundational to the dominant Boeing commercial aircraft assembly philosophy used through the 787 program.

### 29 Citation Count

US2378043A has the highest citation count of all Sorensen patents (29 subsequent patents cite it). Second is US2383225A with 27. Together, the two Oct 24, 1942 filings are his most technically influential work — and they were both filed on the same day, 5 weeks after the first B-24 rolled out.

## US2366365A — Supercharger (Aircraft Power Plant)
**Filed:** Feb 9, 1942 | **Granted:** Jan 2, 1945
**Co-inventor:** None listed
**Class:** 60/599 — Power plants; F02B33/00 — Supercharging; F04D29/58

### What It Covers

Despite the title "Supercharger," this patent is actually about the **complete aircraft power plant integration system** — how to package a V-type liquid-cooled engine with its turbosupercharger, intercooler, intake manifold, and exhaust manifold within a fuselage/nacelle to minimize frontal area and maximize thermal efficiency.

Opening line:
> "This invention relates to **aircraft power plant construction** and is particularly directed to an improved arrangement of the engine, a **turbosupercharger assembly and a fuselage**."

### The Engine Described

The patent describes "an engine of the V type" — liquid-cooled, with:
- Exhaust manifolds on the outer face of each cylinder bank
- Turbosupercharger mounted at the "lower rear quarter" of the engine
- Intercooler positioned in the V between cylinder banks
- Intake reservoir formed below the intercooler, between the V
- All exhaust and intake flows longitudinal (no lateral penetrations of fuselage)

This is specifically a liquid-cooled V-type engine — not a radial. The B-24 used Pratt & Whitney R-1830 Twin Wasp radials. This engine type matches the **Ford GAA V8** (used in M4 Sherman tanks) or a Ford-designed aviation engine. The patent may describe a Ford aircraft engine program parallel to Willow Run that is less documented.

Alternatively: the B-24E/F/G variants used Pratt & Whitney R-1830-43 (radial), but certain test aircraft and AAF programs explored liquid-cooled alternatives. Ford had contracted to produce the Merlin (liquid-cooled V-12) under license from Rolls-Royce — this patent may relate to that program.

### The Paired System with US2397816A

The Supercharger patent explicitly references a co-pending companion:
> "A typical physical embodiment of the impeller turbine combination is described at length in my **copending application, Serial No. 430,118**"

Serial No. 430,118 = US2397816A (Exhaust turbosupercharger), filed the same day (Feb 9, 1942).

The relationship:
- **US2366365A** — Describes the *system*: engine + supercharger + intercooler + fuselage integration, inlet/outlet routing, thermal management
- **US2397816A** — Describes the *mechanism*: the actual turbine/impeller hardware, including the hollow-vaned runner with internal cooling passages

Filed together as an integrated aircraft engine system on February 9, 1942 — 64 days after Pearl Harbor.

### Thermal Management Innovation

A notable engineering insight in the claims:
> "means to so arrange these components that the frontal area of the engine is maintained at a minimum in the interest of streamlining. Also, the intake and exhaust manifolding necessary for the operation of the engine is so arranged that there is a minimum of interference with the aerodynamic qualities of the fuselage."

And countercurrent cooling:
> "careful consideration of the drawings will show that there is a spaced segregation of zones of extreme temperatures, whether these be high or low. Consequently, unwanted heat transfers are prevented without resort to additional insulation. This segregation can be further utilized in connection with the several coolant streams, so that **maximum thermal exchange efficiencies are obtained through full employment of the countercurrent principle**."

This is thermodynamics-aware engineering — the countercurrent principle (hot and cold fluids flowing opposite directions for maximum heat exchange) explicitly stated and engineered into the intercooler. Not a manufacturing patent — an applied engineering/physics patent.

### Signed
> "CHAS. E. SORENSEN."

Sorensen's name alone on the claims — no co-inventors on the system-level patent.

## Cross-Patent Analysis: The Oct 24, 1942 Filing Pair

US2378043A and US2383225A filed same day: one describes the wing section jig, the other describes the factory-level assembly system that the jig fits into. Together they form a complete picture of Willow Run's B-24 assembly architecture as of October 1942 — 5 weeks after the first plane rolled out.

The sequence:
1. First B-24 rolls out — September 1942
2. Sorensen and Smith document the methods that built it — Oct 24, 1942 (2 patents)
3. Those methods are patented before being scaled to mass production
4. Production hits 1/day by March 1943, 1/hour by June 1944

The patent filing is the IP protection step between "proved it works on unit 1" and "scaling to thousands."

## US2472317A — Method of Aircraft Fuselage Construction
**Filed:** May 17, 1943 | **Granted:** Jun 7, 1949
**Co-inventor:** Logan C. Miller
**Class:** B64C1/00; B64C1/06 — Fuselage structure

### What It Covers

Where US2383225A describes the factory-level assembly system and US2378043A describes the wing section jig, US2472317A covers the **fuselage subcomponent assembly method** — how to build a large monocoque fuselage from longitudinal panels (halves, thirds, or quarters) without warping or loss of interchangeability.

The fundamental problem stated in the patent:

> "when the fuselage is broken down into subcomponents much of the rigidity which is found in the fuselage as a whole, because of its tubular, monocoque or semistressed skin construction, is wanting and the subcomponents, as such, are not sufficiently strong to maintain their shape during fabrication and final assembly, and hence are liable to be warped or distorted."

Prior art solution: add structural reinforcement to each panel for handling, then remove it. Problem: dead weight added to the aircraft.

### The Strap Method — Sorensen's Innovation

The patent introduces "straps" — rigid supporting members secured to each subcomponent while still in the jig:

> "In essence, the method comprises the use of supporting and positioning means applied to the various fuselage subcomponents and remaining integral parts thereof from the initial forming of said subcomponents until the completion of the final assembly step."

The strap is secured in the fabrication jig in a precise, predetermined position. It:
1. Supports the subcomponent against warping during handling
2. Carries mating surfaces that locate adjacent panels relative to each other
3. Enables **step-by-step** assembly — panels can be joined two at a time rather than all at once

Key advantage:
> "free access is permitted both beneath and above the pilot's floor which was not possible when, as in the past, it was necessary in order to complete the assembly to bring all the subcomponents involved together at once"

When assembly is complete, all straps are removed:
> "they may then be removed from the completed structure without damage thereto and, of course, adds no weight whatever to the completed ship"

### Fuselage Breakdown — B-24 Application

The patent describes "a typical large-aircraft fuselage" broken into:
- Front fuselage: 5 subcomponents — two side panels, bottom panel, upper front deck, canopy
- Rear fuselage: 3 subcomponents — bottom panel, two side panels
- Tail cone: constructed in one piece separately

The B-24 Liberator fuselage matches this description. The strap rings formed by joining adjacent mating surfaces correspond exactly to the major fuselage frames visible in surviving Willow Run production photographs.

### Cross-Reference Network

US2472317A explicitly cites US2378043A (Sorensen's wing jig patent) and references two companion applications:
- Serial No. 463,268 — "Mating fixture" (noted as "abandoned" in the patent text — the application was not granted)
- The general assembly system described in US2383225A

The four filed together as an integrated aircraft assembly system: factory layout (US2383225A), wing section jig (US2378043A), wing internal construction apparatus (US2428674A — Miller/Scarlett), fuselage panel method (US2472317A).

### Signed
> "CHARLES E. SORENSEN. LOGAN C. MILLER."

## US2428674A — Apparatus for Constructing Aircraft Wings
**Filed:** May 17, 1943 | **Granted:** Oct 7, 1947
**Inventors:** LOGAN C. MILLER + GEO. E. SCARLETT
**Assignee:** FORD MOTOR CO
**Class:** B64F5/00

### Status: NOT a Sorensen Patent

This patent was flagged as a possible 4th Sorensen patent from the May 17, 1943 filing cluster. **Full text confirms it is not.** Inventors are Logan C. Miller and George E. Scarlett. Sorensen's name does not appear.

It is, however, the direct companion to Sorensen's US2378043A. The patent description states explicitly:

> "In some respects it is analogous to copending application Serial No. 463,267, filed October 24, 1942, for Airframe assembly and method thereof, now matured into United States Patent No. 2,378,043, issued June 12, 1945. That invention while principally concerned with the fabrication of the aircraft wings is more particularly concerned with the method of skin assembly and attachment, while the present invention deals with the internal construction of the wing and the structures used to effect this."

**Relationship:**
- US2378043A (Sorensen + Smith, Oct 1942): skin assembly method for the wing — how skin panels are riveted to the skeleton
- US2428674A (Miller + Scarlett, May 1943): wing internal construction — the jig and fixture system for building the internal skeleton before the skin goes on

Together they cover the complete B-24 wing section build sequence, inside out.

### Logan C. Miller — Identified

Miller appears as co-inventor on **both** US2428674A (with Scarlett) and US2472317A (with Sorensen). He is the connecting engineer between the wing internal structure work and the fuselage method work. His role: Willow Run airframe engineering, fuselage and wing fixture design. Not yet found in published Willow Run histories.

### George E. Scarlett — New Identification

Scarlett appears only on US2428674A. His specific focus was the wing internal construction jig. Not yet found in any other Willow Run documentation.

### The May 17, 1943 Filing Cluster — Corrected Count

Four Ford Motor Co patents filed on May 17, 1943. Three are Sorensen's; one is Miller+Scarlett:

| Patent | Inventors | Subject |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| US2378044A | Sorensen, McCarroll, Rothenthaler | Armor plate quenching |
| US2378045A | Sorensen, Van Ranst | Aircraft engine cylinders |
| US2472317A | Sorensen, Miller | Aircraft fuselage construction method |
| US2428674A | Miller, Scarlett | Aircraft wing construction apparatus |

This is the Willow Run engineering team filing their complete B-24 assembly IP in a single coordinated day. Sorensen and four collaborators, three engineering domains: armor, powerplant, airframe.

## Open Questions — Requires Further Research

1. **What engine does US2366365A describe?** V-type liquid-cooled = not B-24's Pratt & Whitney radials. Candidates: Ford Merlin (licensed from Rolls-Royce), Ford GAA V8 derivative, or a Ford prototype aircraft engine. The Merlin connection would be major documentary material — Sorensen patenting the system-level integration of the iconic Spitfire/Mustang engine.

2. **Who is Roscoe M. Smith?** Ann Arbor address. Present on both Oct 24, 1942 patents. Not named in any Willow Run historical narrative currently in the research archive. Possible lead: Ford Motor Company engineering records at Benson Ford Research Center.

3. **Serial No. 463,268 — "Mating fixture"**: Referenced in US2428674A as "copending" and in US2472317A as "abandoned." This application describes how the strap rings are used to mate completed fuselage components to each other. Its abandonment may explain why the mating step appears under-documented in Willow Run technical histories. Worth searching USPTO abandoned application records.

4. **Were any of these patents classified?** WWII manufacturing IP could have been restricted before public filing. The 1945–1949 grant dates (despite 1942–1943 filings) are consistent with wartime review delays, not classification — but worth confirming.

5. **Who is George E. Scarlett?** Co-inventor on US2428674A with Miller. No prior documentation. Possible Ford Motor Co engineering department records at Benson Ford Research Center.
