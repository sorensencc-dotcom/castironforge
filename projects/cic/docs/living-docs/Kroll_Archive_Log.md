## EARLY FORD YEARS (1904–1927)

**1. Henry Ford Museum / thehenryford.org — Group Photo, July 1911 (THF129694)**
Ford Motor Company executives, July 1911. Front row includes 'Chas. Sorensen, P.E. Martin, Gus. Degener, Frank Kulick, Henry Ford, C.H. Wills, Edward Grey, John R. Lee, G.M. MacGregor, Chas. Hartner, Chas. Meida.' CESOR in front row, center-left, alongside Henry Ford — 30 years before Willow Run.
*Significance: Documentary anchor image for the early chapter.*

**2. Detroit Free Press, June 14, 1923 — Rouge Park Bond**
CESOR as 'superintendent of the Ford River Rouge plant' accepts $200,000 in Detroit city bonds as part payment on a $300,000 condemnation judgment for a 1,080-acre park site at River Rouge and West Warren. Underbid New York bond houses, accepting 4.25% bonds.
*Significance: Early civic/financial profile. Good context for the Rouge Park / Sorensen Lake filming location.*

**3. Sorensen vs. Dean Marquis — The Rouge Confrontation (multiple sources, ~1921)**
Three independent sources: (1) CONTENTdm oral history (p.1108): CESOR fired men in Sociological Department; Marquis confronted Henry Ford; 'Marquis got out. I believe he resigned.' (2) Wayne State University Press: When Marquis tried to extend authority to Rouge, CESOR thwarted him. CESOR responded to Marquis calling him an s.o.b. in front of Henry Ford: 'Many times in my life I have been called an s.o.b., but never before or after was I called one by a supposed man of God.' (3) Academic review (Project MUSE): Marquis resigned January 25, 1921 after Ford sided with CESOR.
*Significance: Clearest primary-source example of CESOR's institutional power. Three independent sources confirm outcome.*

**4. THF140538 — The Fifteen Millionth Ford, May 26, 1927**
THF photograph: Henry Ford at the wheel, Edsel Ford, Charles Sorensen, and P.E. Martin in the Fifteen-Millionth Ford Model T on the last day of Model T production.
*Significance: All four key figures in one car at Ford's great pivot moment.*

## DEPRESSION ERA & CIVIC LIFE (1928–1939)

**5. Chenot Circuit Judge Campaign Letter to Sorensen, September 1, 1930 (Ford Archives, Accession 572)**
Personal campaign letter from Prosecuting Attorney James E. Chenot. Executive committee letterhead lists Harry H. Bennett ('Supt. of Service, Ford Motor Co.'), John L. Sorensen, Clyde Ford, and Detroit manufacturing/civic figures. Archive stamp: 'CC#572, Ford Motor Co. #118, Administration Bennett.'
*Significance: CESOR and Bennett operating in parallel in the same political network a decade before their wartime clash.*

**6. Fred Black Testimony — Edsel at the DIA, ~1932 (typed transcript)**
Edsel to Black: 'It's got to the point where I'm paying the salary of the Director and the staff, and if we're going to keep the place open I'm going to have to pay the salaries of the guards and the janitors.'
*Significance: Primary source for Edsel's private exhaustion. Background character material for the Edsel arc.*

**7. Fred Black Testimony — Rivera Controversy (typed transcript)**
Inside account of manufactured Rivera mural controversy as a deliberate DIA publicity strategy. Edsel knew and approved: 'thought it was a great scheme.'
*Significance: Documents sophisticated, risk-tolerant Edsel operating in parallel to CESOR's world.*

**8. DIA Administrative Staff Salary Sheet**
Internal document: original payroll $72,680; reduced total after cuts: $29,996.70. Staff 'Dropped.' Valentiner and Burroughs on Edsel's personal money.
*Significance: Financial document confirming Edsel personally funding DIA staff.*

**9. Edsel Ford / Mayor Frank Murphy Correspondence, March 1933**
Edsel to Murphy (March 2, 1933) declining reappointment to Arts Commission — 'pressing demands upon my time.' Murphy expressing 'deep regret' after eight years of service.
*Significance: Primary documents of Edsel's withdrawal from civic life at the banking crisis moment.*

**10. Mike Kroll Synthesis Posts — Depression/DIA Episode (Facebook)**
Kroll's analytical argument: Edsel 'doesn't withdraw from responsibility under pressure. He concentrates it.' On the Depression: 'The Liebold testimony establishes personal financial exposure at scale. The Black testimony establishes intentional use of publicity under financial strain.'
*Significance: Most useful as researcher's framing document — analytical scaffolding for the Edsel arc.*

**11. Detroit Free Press / Lansing State Journal — Multi-Year Salary Disclosures, 1936–1941**
1936: CESOR at $103,435 as 'general superintendent,' outpacing Edsel ($90,036 as president). 1937: CESOR at $158,599, surpassing Edsel ($137,564). 1939: CESOR at $166,071. 1941: CESOR at $220,004.96, second-highest in the auto industry.
*Significance: Multi-year record establishing CESOR frequently exceeding the nominal president.*

## DEFENSE BUILDUP (1940–1941)

**12. Automotive News, July 29, 1940 — Ford Motor Co. Directory Listing**
Lists 'C. E. Sorensen, prod. mgr.' alongside Edsel B. Ford as president and treasurer.
*Significance: Contemporaneous evidence of CESOR's exact title at the outset of the defense buildup.*

**13. Lansing State Journal / Detroit Free Press, June 11–12, 1940 — Edsel, CESOR, & Knudsen Defense Photo**
AP wirephoto: Edsel Ford, William S. Knudsen, and Charles E. Sorensen conferring on airplane motor production. Inspected a Navy pursuit ship and traveled to Washington within 24 hours for conference on air rearmament motor selection.
*Significance: One of the most widely published photographs of CESOR. Pre-Pearl Harbor timing historically significant.*

**14. Detroit Free Press, September 18, 1940 — $11M Airplane Engine Plant Groundbreaking**
Edsel Ford at the controls of a steam shovel at the Rouge groundbreaking for $11M airplane engine plant. CESOR and P.E. Martin standing by. Plant to produce 4,000 Pratt & Whitney motors. Plant is windowless — built for blackout conditions.
*Significance: Pre-Willow Run visual documentation of CESOR at Ford's first wartime aviation commitment.*

**15. Detroit Free Press, July 18, 1941 — CESOR Named Ford Director**
Formal announcement with published portrait photograph. CESOR named to Ford Motor Company Board of Directors alongside A.M. Wibel. General manager since 1912.
*Significance: Portrait photograph and directorship at the peak of institutional standing — months before Willow Run's first bomber.*

**16. Ford Archives Accession 796 — C.E. Sorensen Papers, 'Fifty Thousand Airplanes Per Year'**
Box 15A: 'Longhand and typescripts of C.E. Sorensen's compilation of data re. Ford's program for mass production of bombers... Title: 50,000 Airplanes Per Year. Can It Be Done? Yes, Emphatically Yes. Why Be So Sure About It?' Memo reporting conversation with Mr. Pioch at Consolidated Aircraft Corp. in San Diego, 1/21/41.
*Significance: CESOR's own visionary document making the case for mass bomber production before Willow Run was approved. Title alone is documentary gold.*

**17. Reminiscences p.56 — San Diego Assignment (CONTENTdm)**
Around Feb/early March 1941, CESOR called the speaker in to form a team to go to San Diego to learn B-24 production from Consolidated. Left March 4, 1941.
*Significance: Corroborates Willow Run timeline. Shows CESOR's method of sudden, direct assignment.*

**18. Bricker Oral History — Willow Run Site Selection & San Diego Team Dispatch, March 1941 (Ford Archives, p.71)**
Three reasons for Willow Run site: 1,878 acres, security (less sabotage danger), proximity to New York Central Railway. Albert Kahn's organization designed the building. CESOR and Bricker personally selected top manufacturing men and dispatched them to San Diego.
*Significance: Primary source on practical reasoning behind Willow Run's location.*

**19. Ford Motor Company UAW-CIO Departmental Communication, May 26, 1941 (THF273868)**
Edsel Ford's President's Office assembles labor negotiation board after Rouge strike settlement. Copies to: B.J. Craig, P.E. Martin, C.E. Sorensen, M.L. Bricker, R.R. Rausch.
*Significance: CESOR copied but not a board member — consistent with Willow Run focus.*

## WILLOW RUN — ACTIVE YEARS (1941–1944)

**20. Willow Run Timeline Document (internal Ford binder)**
Chronological log of Willow Run milestones July 26, 1941 through early 1942. Entry for Oct. 22, 1941 (underlined): 'First official landing at Willow Run Airport made by Major Doolittle, C. E. Sorensen, and H. P. Henning.'
*Significance: Primary Ford Motor Company document placing CESOR's name at the birth of the airfield.*

**21. Meigs Naming Memo (October 3, 1941)**
Merrill C. Meigs memo cc'd Edsel Ford and CESOR requesting official rename from 'Ypsilanti unit' to 'Willow Run Bomber Plant of the Ford Motor Company.'
*Significance: The document that officially named Willow Run.*

**22. Henry Ford Finding Aids Screenshot (findingaids.thehenryford.org)**
Charles E. Sorensen Office Files and Personal Records Subseries, 1915–1945 (Accession 38). Owen W. Bombard Interviews (Accession 65) with CESOR as named subject. Nevins/Hill Research Series (Accession 572).
*Significance: Maps primary archival collections at THF. Research roadmap for the documentary team.*

**23. Ypsilanti Daily Press, April 21, 1942 — Full-Page Community Ad**
Hartwick-Wescott (lumber) congratulatory ad: 'We congratulate you Mr. Henry Ford and Mr. Edsel Ford and Mr. Charles Sorensen and Mr. Roscoe Smith and Mr. Logan Miller and all the other men whose science and skill have made possible this greatest of Bomber factories.'
*Significance: Community-level recognition naming CESOR alongside Henry and Edsel Ford.*

**24. The Herald-Press / The Daily Telegram, May 21, 1942 — 'First Big Bomber Rolls From Willow Run Lines'**
CESOR quoted: '...all of the sub-assemblies such as the wings, fuselage sections and tail surfaces on this first plane will fit the 1,000th plane of the same design turned out in this plant... the Willow Run plant has been built on an estimated production rate of one bomber every hour.'
*Significance: CESOR's own words making the mass-production-as-car-building analogy explicit. Key thematic quote.*

**25. Detroit Evening Times, June 7, 1942 — 'War Production Leaders of Three Nations Appraising Willow Run'**
On airport apron: W.A. Harriman, C.E. Sorensen, Donald M. Nelson (WPB chief), Henry Ford, Oliver Lyttleton (British production chief), W.S. Robinson (Australian production expert), Ernest Kanzler, Edsel Ford, M.L. Bricker.
*Significance: The single most powerful group photograph. Three nations' production leadership assembled at CESOR's plant.*

**26. Truman & Sorensen Photo (THF116362)**
Henry Ford Museum: Sen. Harry S. Truman and Charles Sorensen with B-24 at Willow Run, 1942. Truman's signature visible. Truman visited as head of the Senate war production investigation committee.
*Significance: Future president + CESOR under a B-24 propeller. Truman's committee was scrutinizing Willow Run's production struggles.*

**27. Willow Run Group Photo, June 12, 1942 (THF, accession 76811-6-12-42)**
Group photo at Willow Run Airport: Gen. William S. Knudsen (6th from left), Edsel Ford (8th), Charles E. Sorensen (9th). ~15 men total in front of a B-24. Stamp reads 6-12-42 (confirmed June 12, 1942 — Knudsen visit week).
*Significance: Strong visual — Knudsen, Edsel, and CESOR together at Willow Run.*

**28. Detroit Public Library — Sorensen / Knudsen / Edsel Ford Signed Photo, June 12, 1942**
14x11 archival print (DPL, Resource ID: na020044). Handwritten: 'To Gen. Knudsen, best wishes, Chas. E. Sorensen, 6/12/42. To my friend, General Wm. Knudsen, Edsel Ford.' Both autographs present.
*Significance: Primary document as much as photograph — CESOR's and Edsel's signatures together on a wartime image, inscribed to the head of US war production.*

**29. Freedom Team Photo (August 1942)**
Group photo at Willow Run Airport: Gen. Knudsen (center, uniform), Edsel Ford (left), Charles E. Sorensen (white arrow). Dated 8/1942.
*Significance: High-value documentary visual.*

**30. Col. Brandt Letter to Edsel Ford, December 23, 1942 (War Dept/AAF)**
Army Air Forces resident rep at Willow Run writes to Edsel about B-24E progress. Invites Edsel — or if unavailable, 'Sorensen or Bricker or Logan Miller' — to Jan. 11 San Diego meeting. Henry Ford suggests Lindbergh might attend.
*Significance: Rare direct military-to-Edsel communication. Shows CESOR as Edsel's functional deputy.*

**31. Dearborn Historical Museum — Christman Oral History**
Cafeteria supervisor. Describes CESOR's 47-foot yacht (referred to as 'Henry II'), Henry Ford occasionally coming aboard, 1942 government requisition for Alaskan survey. 'In 1942 the Willow Run Bomber Plant was built under the supervision of Mr. Sorensen.' 'He could not have been nicer if he had been my own father.'
*Significance: Third-party corroboration of CESOR's supervision. Strong character testimony from an unlikely source.*

**32. Robinson Reminiscences — Robot Bomb (CONTENTdm)**
Col. Keirn of Wright Field called CESOR asking if Ford would build a robot bomb. Traces back to 1918 when Gen. Arnold (then a captain) saw a Ford-built robot bomb with Henry Ford Sr.
*Significance: WWII sidebar connecting Ford's WWI and WWII military work.*

**33. Robinson Reminiscences — M7 Director (p.162, CONTENTdm)**
Edsel Ford, CESOR, Wibel, Bricker conferred frequently with Mr. Treese on M7 director production. First unit completed June 16, 1942. CESOR calls the author directly to come to Highland Park immediately.
*Significance: CESOR's hands-on wartime command style.*

**34. Reminiscences — Fuselage on Its Nose (pp. 74–75, CONTENTdm)**
CESOR insisted B-24 fuselages should be built standing on their nose. Douglas Aircraft engineers secretly brought in to refute it — one said it 'isn't worth a damn.' Production set up conventionally.
*Significance: Documentary anecdote: CESOR's audacity and unconventional thinking. Cinematic.*

**35. Reminiscences — Roscoe Smith & Willow Run Power Struggle (CONTENTdm)**
Roscoe Smith was first in charge at Willow Run (1941). Known trouble between Smith, CESOR, Bricker, and Bennett. Smith removed; Bricker took over. Army wanted single authority; Henry Ford picked Bricker.
*Significance: Internal power struggle central to the documentary narrative. Confirms CESOR was not the final authority.*

**36. H. Hicks Reminiscences (Ford Archives / CONTENTdm — combined)**
Hicks credits Edsel's quiet moral influence for retaining talented people. 'Henry Ford liked Sorensen because he had a way of getting things done that Mr. Ford wanted done. He always realized that Henry Ford was the boss.' CESOR's 'pencil trick' on the dynamometer: setting a pencil on end to demonstrate engine balance. Knudsen famously stood on a running engine, not realizing it was running, it was so smooth.
*Significance: Third-party characterization of all three members of the CESOR/Henry/Edsel dynamic. Pencil trick and Knudsen on engine are cinematic anecdotes.*

**37. Lepine Reminiscences (pp. 67 & 69)**
Edsel Ford called Henry Ford 'Father' (never 'Dad'), called CESOR 'Charlie,' called Fred Black 'Fred.' CESOR and Mayo used Edsel's first name.
*Significance: Intimate detail about workplace relationships.*

**38. Howard Simpson Reminiscences (Ford Archives)**
Filed under 'Ford, Edsel — Character & Business Methods.' Edsel-Sorensen dynamic as 'very cool' — Sorensen would 'throw cold water' on anything Edsel took interest in to humiliate him. Also describes Sorensen's 'bludgeoning approach.'
*Significance: Primary source characterization of CESOR's personality and fraught relationship with Edsel. Highly quotable.*

**39. Bricker Oral History — CESOR's Physical Management Style (Ford Archives)**
CESOR would physically grab people but 'would never kick anybody.' Bricker saved CESOR from being beaten up multiple times. 'He was very disliked all through the plant.' 'From 1921 forward... Mr. Sorensen is in ascendancy.'
*Significance: Primary source testimony on CESOR's physical management style and institutional standing.*

**40. Ford: Decline and Rebirth (Nevins & Hill), pp. 162 & 164**
April 1941 Rouge strike: Henry Ford met CESOR at Willow Run on April 2. CESOR sent to Miami; Edsel came back from Florida only to be told to leave it to Bennett. On April 11, Henry Ford telephoned CESOR who jotted in his diary: 'Strike settled.'
*Significance: CESOR's diary cited as primary source for strike resolution. Documents CESOR/Henry agreed on militant response Edsel ultimately overruled.*

**41. Departmental Communication, May 12, 1943**
Ford distribution list showing C.E. Sorensen — Manufacturing alongside Bricker (Willow Run), Rausch (Superintendent's Office), and Army Air Forces personnel.
*Significance: Organizational snapshot from mid-war, just before CESOR's departure.*

**42. Automotive News, May 31, 1943 — Tribute to Edsel Ford**
Full-page obituary coverage. Detroit mourners list led by 'Charles E. Sorensen.' Testimonials from Knudsen, UAW president R.J. Thomas ('I am very sorry to hear of the death of Edsel Ford').
*Significance: CESOR leads the Detroit mourners list. The UAW president's condolence — given the labor history — is striking.*

**43. Gregorie Interview (Dropbox PDF / Ford Archives)**
E.T. 'Bob' Gregorie recounts Edsel's final visit to the design studio in Spring 1943. On the Lincoln acquisition (1922): CESOR and his production group referred to Edsel as 'the boy.'
*Significance: Last sighting of Edsel by his chief designer. Reveals CESOR's circle's contempt from an insider with no ax to grind.*

**44. Arsenal of Democracy (2014), pp. 120–121 + Notes p. 314**
Bennett/Edsel physical confrontation: Bennett announces kidnap plot; Edsel snaps ('Stop this talk!'); Bennett tears off coat and charges; CESOR pushes Edsel out the door. CESOR's memoir (acc 65, box 69, p. 912): 'Never was any business run like the Ford empire. If a story was written as a fairy tale the public would reject it as too fantastic to believe.'
*Significance: The confrontation scene with CESOR as Edsel's protector. Benson Ford archival citation is retrievable.*

**45. Bricker Oral History — Post-Edsel Board Composition, June 1, 1943 (Ford Archives, pp. 116 & 82)**
Org chart: Henry Ford, President; C.E. Sorensen, Vice President in Charge of Production; Mrs. Eleanor Ford, Director; H.H. Bennett, Director; M.L. Bricker, Director and General Manager, Bomber Plant. Henry Ford II elected Vice President January 22, 1944.
*Significance: CESOR's formal title at peak power — and Bennett's simultaneous elevation to equal Director status, triggering the final conflict.*

**46. Bricker Oral History — 'Sorensen began to slip in Ford's mind about 1942' (Ford Archives, p. 82)**
'I think he began to slip in Mr. Ford's mind about 1942, about the time the bomber plant started. He found out that Sorensen was making promises in production that he couldn't live up to.' Knudsen criticized the thousand planes a day claim. Actual promise: one per hour, sixteen per day — which they achieved. Final total: 8,685 four-engined bombers.
*Significance: Eyewitness account of the precise moment CESOR lost Henry Ford's confidence.*

**47. My Forty Years with Ford (archive.org) — Combined Entry**
(a) p. 310: Edsel's financial toll from 1933 banking crisis — 'at a cost of between six and seven million dollars' — 'his health went with it.' (b) Edsel's death chapter — CESOR's notes from phone call with Henry Ford in 1943; Ford demanded Wibel's dismissal; Edsel's final conversation with CESOR ('If you go, I go too'); Henry Ford's cognitive decline — 'hardened into an obsession which occasionally flared into hallucination.' Footnote: 'Henry's greatest failure was his treatment of his only son Edsel... and this treatment may have hastened his son's death.'
*Significance: CESOR's own words on the most dramatic moments of his Ford career. The emotional core of the documentary.*

**48. Willow Run Plant Data Bulletin, Vol. I No. 63, November 19, 1943**
Item 299 FLYAWAYS: Nov. 16, 1943 — new record of 20 ships flown away in a single day, one every 11 minutes. Item 300 QUALITY CONTROL: 'Since the establishment of the Quality Control Department several weeks ago, the average squawks per ship have been reduced from approximately 130 to 60.'
*Significance: The CESOR-era QC system at peak performance — six weeks before CESOR was pushed out.*

**49. Original Willow Run Squawk Chart (archival binder graph)**
'Average Total Squawks Per Ship Per Month,' Aug 1943–May 1945. ~115 squawks (Aug 1943) declining steeply to ~40 (Jan–Feb 1944). Asterisk/star mark at approximately Feb 1944.
*Significance: Primary source squawk graph. The Feb 1944 marker likely indicates the CESOR departure. The summer 1944 bump is visible — management disruption made measurable.*

**50. Bricker Oral History — CESOR's Counter-Bennett Maneuver: The Gillespie Gambit (Ford Archives, p. 100)**
CESOR brought John Gillespie into the company specifically as a counterweight against Harry Bennett. Built the case through Bill Cameron, got Cameron to tell Henry Ford that Gillespie would be 'a great man to put up against Bennett.' Ford hired Gillespie. Bennett physically knocked him down several times. The gambit failed.
*Significance: CESOR actively maneuvering against Bennett through back-channel political moves — and losing.*

## CESOR'S DEPARTURE (MARCH 1944)

**51. Willow Run Production Log Entry, March 4, 1944 (Ford Archives / HFM)**
The plant's own daily chronology: 'Charles E. Sorensen, Ford Vice-President, resigned today.' One line buried between wing completions and master change ships. Ship #1800 completed; open house ~52,000 visitors on Feb. 27.
*Significance: The plant's contemporaneous record of the firing. Cinematic contrast: one line among production statistics.*

**52. Buffalo News, March 4, 1944**
CESOR's resignation announcement from Miami Beach — resigned 'with much regret' as VP of Ford, citing need for rest, expected to return to Detroit around May 1.
*Significance: Press documentation of the Ford departure.*

**53. Detroit Free Press, March 5, 1944 — 'Sorensen Out as Production Chief at Ford's'**
Full front-page with portrait photo captioned 'Out after 39 years.' The firing came via telephone from Henry Ford's Georgia winter home while CESOR was fishing in the Bahamas. His 1941 salary: $220,004.96, 'exceeded in the auto industry only by those of C.E. Wilson, president of General Motors Corp., and the late Edsel B. Ford.'
*Significance: The documentary's central turning point in full contemporaneous detail.*

**54. Detroit Free Press, March 6, 1944 — 'Dismissal of Sorensen No Surprise to Capital'**
Washington insiders confirm CESOR had been effectively sidelined since 'last October' [1943], retaining VP title but taking 'no actual part in the conduct of company affairs.' CESOR announced 'resignation' from Miami Beach; Henry Ford at Ways, GA; Bennett at Palm Springs, CA.
*Significance: Washington's view of the firing as long-anticipated. The geography is cinematically evocative.*

## POST-DEPARTURE & LEGACY (1944–1946+)

**55. Willow Run Production Table, 1944–1945 (archival document)**
Month-by-month: Jan 1944: 210 final assembly, 35,644 employees. Peak: Aug 1944 — 432 final assembly, 26,283 employees. By June 1945: 86 final assembly, 6,871 employees.
*Significance: Primary production record for the post-CESOR period.*

**56. Willow Run Labor Disruptions, July–September 1944 (multiple newspapers)**
July: 200 crane operators walked out — 18,000 workers idled. July 17: 31,000 return after unanimous vote. Sept 8: 15,000 sent home after 20 riveters (mostly women) refused transfer.
*Significance: Contemporaneous press record of Bennett-era labor disruptions.*

**57. Mike Kroll Squawk Analysis — 'Tales from the Bomber Plant: Squawks 1944' (Facebook posts)**
Two-part analysis. Part 2: 'The rise in squawks... is the intersection of a reduced workforce, a changing aircraft, a disrupted line and a management system that had shifted from coordination to control.' Bennett's method: 'Decisions enforced. Not negotiated.' By early 1945, Henry II takes control, stoppages subside, squawks fall.
*Significance: The documentary's implicit counterfactual: what Willow Run lost when CESOR left.*

**58. Gen. H.H. Arnold Visit to Willow Run, July 13–14, 1944 (Ann Arbor News + Times Herald + DFP)**
Gen. 'Hap' Arnold made surprise visit, autographed a bomber with Henry Ford. Looking on: H.H. Bennett, M.L. Bricker, Henry Ford. Arnold arrived the day before the plant's first shutdown.
*Significance: The Army's vote of confidence — and its timing. Bennett and Bricker are the faces of Ford management, not CESOR.*

**59. THF146304 — Harry Bennett at Navy Service School Groundbreaking, Ford Rouge Plant, 1943**
THF photograph: Ray Rausch (center) and Harry Bennett (right, white suit and straw hat) at groundbreaking for swimming pool at Navy Service School.
*Significance: Visual of Bennett as civilian power center at Ford during the war period.*

**60. AAF/AMC Internal Report (1946) — CESOR as 'Principal Figure' at Willow Run**
Army Air Forces/Air Materiel Command (IPS, 1946): 'Sorensen whose production ability played a big part in the tremendous task of tooling up for airplane mass-production became the principal figure in plant operations... A tough production man with little sympathy for anything that threatened to interfere with production...' CESOR left largely due to AAF complaints and Bennett rivalry following Edsel's death.
*Significance: The US government's own verdict on CESOR's role and departure. 'Principal figure' is a direct quote from the Army.*
